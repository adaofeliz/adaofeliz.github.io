The hardest habits to stop are the ones nobody sees.

No one is watching. No one is auditing. No one is asking why. And the way out starts with something that feels like a lie.

## The habit with no witness

Most habits people try to change have an audience. When I started going to the gym, people noticed. When I lost weight, people commented. When I fell off, I felt it in their silence as much as in my own body. That kind of feedback is loud, and loud feedback carries you further than you think.

But a lot of habits are not like that. They live in the gaps. The phone picked up without a reason. The snack nobody counts. The tab opened on autopilot. The small thing done in the minutes between one thing and the next. Nobody sees them, so nobody questions them. And when nobody questions something, it slowly stops looking like a choice. It just looks like how things are.

That is the trap. A habit nobody sees has no friction. It does not cost anything in front of anyone. The only person who can audit it is the same person who wants to keep doing it.

The judge and the defendant. Same person. Same room.

## The machine underneath

I went looking for why this is so hard. The research humbled me a bit.

A [study](https://www.ucl.ac.uk/news/2025/may/how-brain-forms-habits-dual-learning-system) from the Sainsbury Wellcome Centre at UCL, published in Nature in 2025, found a second learning system in the brain. The first one is the one everybody knows. Do something, get a reward, do it again. The second one does not care about the reward at all. It reinforces an action simply because it was repeated. The more predictable the action becomes, the more the brain treats it as the default.

That landed hard.

It means a habit is not only kept alive by the pleasure it gives. It is kept alive by the repetition itself. Even when the thing stopped feeling good a long time ago, the machine keeps running. Not because anyone wants it. Because it happened yesterday.

So a habit is not deleted the moment someone decides to stop. Deciding changes nothing in the machine. Something else has to be built on top of it, repeated enough times that it becomes the new default.

## Pretending to sleep

Somewhere, a long time ago, I read a line about sleep that never left me. I tried to find the source again and could not. The idea was simple. To fall asleep, you first have to pretend you are sleeping.

I think about what that actually means. You lie down. You close your eyes. You stay very still. Your breathing slows. Your mind is still awake, still racing, still nowhere near sleep. But your body is already doing everything a sleeping body does. It is faking it.

And then, at some point nobody ever notices, the fake becomes real. Nobody decides to fall asleep. It cannot be decided. It can only be pretended well enough, for long enough, that the real thing arrives.

That is the whole process, as far as I can tell.

Not one big decision. Not willpower. Pretending to already be someone who does not do the thing. Acting like that person in the exact moments where the old habit used to show up. It feels fake. It feels like lying. The mind is still awake, still pulling toward the old default, while the body goes through the motions of the new one.

## Acting as if

It turns out this is an old idea. Alfred Adler, the psychologist, built a therapeutic technique in the 1920s that he called [acting as if](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fake_it_till_you_make_it). William James is often quoted with the same thought: if you want a quality, act as if you already had it. The behaviour comes first. The identity follows.

I used to dislike this idea. Fake it until you make it always sounded to me like something said on a stage, by someone selling something. Hollow. A little dishonest.

I see it differently now.

Pretending is not lying when the only audience is yourself. It is rehearsal. It is lying down in the dark, eyes closed, still, waiting. Not pretending so someone else believes it. Pretending so the machine underneath has something new to repeat.

## The quiet audit

The last piece is the audit.

A habit nobody sees still needs a witness. When there is no one else, the witness has to be the person in the mirror the next morning. Not to judge. Just to notice. I wrote about [lowering the bar until I could clear it](https://www.adaofeliz.com/blog/when-discipline-falls-apart) when discipline fell apart in the gym. This feels like the same lesson, from the other side. Small. Quiet. Repeated. Nobody clapping.

The old pull does not disappear. The research is clear on that too. The old pathway stays. It just stops being the one that wins.

Lie down. Close the eyes. Stay still.

Sooner or later, the pretending stops being pretending.
